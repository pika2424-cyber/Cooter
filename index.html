Welcome 👋

Cooter lists the native plants, animals and fungi recorded around you, grouped by type of life. It pulls records from iNaturalist and the Atlas of Living Australia, and looks up the Ecological Vegetation Class (EVC) you're standing in from Victoria's DEECA vegetation mapping.

  1. Press Use my location (or click the map).
  2. Pick a radius and press Find native species.
  3. Tick ✔ species you've seen and ☆ the ones you want to find.

EVC data covers Victoria only. Species data works anywhere in Australia.

⚠️ Before you eat anything wild
  • Only eat a plant if you are 100% sure of its identity. Several edible species have toxic look-alikes, and some need preparation (e.g. leaching or cooking) or are only edible in part.
  • Collecting plants in national parks, state parks and most reserves is illegal. Protected flora need permits under the FFG Act. Ask permission on private land.
  • Bush foods are part of First Nations cultural knowledge. Harvest lightly, never take the whole plant, and consider learning from or buying through Aboriginal-led bush food businesses.
  • A plant shows here only if Wikipedia records food use for it (a food category, or a sentence describing it as food). An absence doesn't mean a plant is inedible, and inclusion doesn't mean the local form is safe.

How it works

Data sources

  • iNaturalist – community observations. Uses iNaturalist's native establishment flags, so only species recognised as native to the area are shown.
  • Atlas of Living Australia (ALA) – aggregates the Victorian Biodiversity Atlas, museum collections, eBird, BirdLife and more. Records flagged as introduced or cultivated are removed, as are species iNaturalist lists as introduced in the area.
  • ALA Non-Native Species List for Australia – removes any species listed as introduced in your state, or exotic to Australia altogether.
  • Wikipedia – species usage passages and edible plant categories (CC BY-SA).
  • EVC mapping – DEECA's NV2005_EVCBCS (current native vegetation) and NV1750_EVCBCS (pre-European vegetation) layers from DataVic, including each EVC's Bioregional Conservation Status.

EVC patch search

"Only my EVC patch" takes the mapped vegetation polygon you're standing in, clips it to your radius, and searches ALA inside that exact shape. iNaturalist doesn't support polygon searches, so its results use the patch's bounding box.

If you're standing somewhere with no remnant vegetation (e.g. a suburb or farm paddock), Cooter shows the pre-1750 EVC – what grew there originally – which is a great guide for indigenous gardening and revegetation.

Rarity

  • Listed status – Victorian Flora and Fauna Guarantee Act threatened list and the national EPBC Act list (via ALA), plus IUCN Red List (via iNaturalist).
  • Local rarity – how few records a species has in your search area compared with other species in the same group. "Very rare here" means only a single record.

Local rarity reflects how often things are recorded, which also depends on how easy they are to spot and identify, so treat it as a guide.

Species uses

Each species' details include Traditional (Indigenous) usage and Other documented usage (Western & international). Both are quoted directly from the species' English Wikipedia article, with a link to the section each passage came from.

  • A passage counts as traditional usage only if a sentence mentions Aboriginal, Torres Strait Islander or other Indigenous peoples and a use. Uses by Indigenous peoples outside Australia are included where the article names them (food, fibre, medicine, tools…).
  • Other usage comes only from use-type sections (Uses, Cultivation, Timber, Culinary…) and must state a use.
  • Nothing is generated or filled in. If the article has no usage information, Cooter says so. Much traditional knowledge isn't written on Wikipedia and belongs to Traditional Owners.

Edible plants

The Edible plants tab lists native plants in your search area that Wikipedia documents as food. This means either a food category (Bushfood, Edible fruits, Edible nuts and seeds, Leaf vegetables, Root vegetables…) or a sentence describing the plant as edible or eaten, which is shown as evidence. Plants without this evidence aren't listed. Always confirm identification before eating anything, and never harvest threatened species or from parks and reserves.

Mycorrhizal fungi

The 🍄 button in a plant's details lists fungi recorded as its mycorrhizal partners in GloBI (Global Biotic Interactions), with partners recorded near you shown first. Origin comes from iNaturalist and the ALA Non-Native Species List, and edibility and uses come from Wikipedia. Missing data is shown as missing.

Management practice

The Management tab turns your search results into site advice. It looks for:

  • Species groups with shared needs: hollow-dependent animals, ground-dwelling mammals, woodland birds, raptors, frogs, aquatic fauna, waterbirds, reptiles, orchids, grassland wildflowers, invertebrates, fungi and threatened plants. Most groups are triggered only when a threatened or locally rare member is present. Frogs, orchids, aquatic fauna and hollow users are triggered by any record.
  • Keystone species: eucalypts, wattles, Sweet Bursaria, banksias, grass-trees, sheoaks, Kangaroo Grass, mistletoe, flying-foxes, digging marsupials, echidnas, wombats, lyrebirds, platypus, burrowing crayfish, Powerful Owls and quolls. It also flags Noisy Miners as a "problem native".
  • The EVC habitat group, which sets indicative fire intervals and grazing and revegetation guidance.

From these it rates 19 common activities (burning, slashing, firewood collection, herbicide, grazing, pest control and more) as Avoid, Moderate, Proceed with care or Encourage. It also lists actions to increase populations, each with the species that benefit. This is general guidance, not a site assessment.

Using "Records since 2000" in More options helps remove old and vagrant records from the advice.
